@ The University of Southern California began operation 
with 53 students in 1880 on land donated by a Catholic, a 
Jew, and a Protestant. Nearly 1,000 persons, almost one- 
tenth of the population of Los Angeles, were present for the 
laying of the cornerstone on September 4, 1880. 

@ USC, the oldest major private co-educational university 
in the West, is non-sectarian. 

@ The first USC building, Widney Hall, was erected at a 
cost of $5,060 and furnished for $1,200. It is still in use by 
the School of Music. It is the oldest university building in 
southern California, and is Historical Landmark No, 536 of 
the California State Parks Commission. 


@ In its early years, USC was primarily a college of liberal 
arts, but it soon became a major university. USC now consists 
of the College of Letters, Arts and Sciences; the Graduate 
School; eighteen professional or specialized schools, each with 
its own dean or director and faculty; plus Summer Session 
and University College, for late afternoon and evening classes. 

@ USC's eighteen schools are Architecture and Fine Arts, 
Business Administration, Dentistry, Education, Engineering, In- 
ternational and Public Affairs, Journalism, Law, Library Sci- 
ence, Medicine, Music, Performing Arts, Pharmacy, Philosophy, 
Politics and International Relations, Public Administration, 
Religion, and Social Work. 


THE UNIVERSITY TODAY 


@ Under the leadership of its seventh President, Dr. 
Norman Topping, USC has achieved in whole or in a large 
part the major academic objectives of its Master Plan for 
enterprise and excellence in education. The plan’s major 
objective was and is a high concentration of superior students, 
with a maximum enrollment of nearly 20,000 and the 
necessary physical expansion to accommodate them. 

@ The USC faculty numbers 1,140 fulltime, 1,030 parttime, 


4 


and 2,255 volunteer medical faculty. Doctorates are held by 
nearly 90 percent of fulltime faculty in Letters, Arts, and 
Sciences, far above the national average. 

® Student enrollment in the fall of 1968 reached 10,719 
fulltime and 8,879 parttime. Sixty-four percent of degrees 
awarded in 1967-68 were graduate or professional degrees. 

@ New entrance requirements have resulted in the ad- 
mission of a succession of the finest freshmen classes ever 
admitted in the history of the University. 

@ The total of degree-holding alumni now exceeds 118,- 
000. Eighty percent of USC’s alumni live in southern Cali- 
fornia. 

@ University receipts in fiscal 1967-68 were $67,542,582. 
Only 34% of this came from student fees. Forty-five percent 
came from gifts, grants and contracts, 1% from endowment, 
and 20% from auxiliary enterprises, sales and services of 
educational departments, and other sources. 

@ Sixty-seven percent of income is expended for instruc- 
tion and research; 10% for administration and general 
expenses; 5% for student aid; 6% for clinic and dispensary; 
4% for plant operation and maintenance and 8% for aux- 
iliary enterprises. 

@ Capital investment has reached $97,423,300; endow- 
ment is now nearly $31 million. 

@ USC's campus includes approximately 111 acres and 
more than 73 permanent buildings. More buildings have 
been erected on the campus during the era of the Master 
Plan {since 1961) than were built during the first 81 years 
of USC's history. 

@ USC has raised more than $153 million during the 
Master Plan era—$105,620,000 of that sum having come 
from private sources. The original Master Plan announcement 
called for raising $106,675,000—from all sources. 

@ Now completed is the first unit of what will be a 
multi-million-dollar Marine Science Center on Catalina Island. 
The National Science Foundation and USC have put more than 
$1 million into the first building, a marine biology laboratory, 
and first classes were taught at the Center during the 
summer. 

@ A team of four USC students won the General Electric 
College Bowl competition on nationwide television in 1968, 
becoming only the 23rd of more than 300 college and uni- 
versity team in 10 years to win this honor and more than 
$19,000 in scholarship funds. USC Trustees added $24,500 
and the total fund supports students from minority groups. 

@ The Martin Luther King Memorial Scholarships were 
established with a $50,000 gift from Anna Bing Arnold, 
a member of the USC Board of Trustees. 

@ Among the growing honor roll of distinguished alumni, 
USC can count the wife of the President of the United States, 
Patricia Ryan Nixon; the secretary of health, education and 
welfare, Robert H. Finch; and two astronauts, Walter M. 
Schirra and Neil A. Armstrong. 

® Drawing from its advantage of being a major urban 
university and responding to the certainty of mushrooming 
urbanization in the nation, USC has established a Center of 
Urban Affairs that focuses the University's inter-disciplinary 
resources on urban problems and offers the nation’s first pro- 
grams to lead the B.A., M.A., and Ph.D. degrees in Urban 
studies. The Center also conducts a revolutionary undergrad- 
vate program, the Urban Semester, designed to train  stu- 
ents to “think urban’’ through confrontations with virtually 
all aspects of the megalopolis and by research of their own 
choosing.

DR. NORMAN TOPPING 
University President 


Norman Topping, M.D., is in 
his twelfth year as the seventh 
president of the University of 
Southern California. He took 
office on Sept. 1, 1958, and is 
the second alumnus to become 
the chief executive officer of 
the oldest and largest inde- 
pendent coeducational Univer- 
sity in the American West. 


He was graduated from 
USC with a Bachelor of Arts 
degree in 1933 and with the 
Doctor of Medicine degree in 
1936. 


His years at the helm of USC have seen the University’s 
Master Plan for enterprise and excellence in education, an- 
nounced May 17, 1961, take shape and move rapidly toward 
fulfillment. Many of the Master Plan's goals for academic en- 
richment and physical development already have been at- 
tained and others are firmly in progress. 


Distinguished men of learning have been attracted to USC 
to add their knowledge and ability to that already being ap- 
plied by USC's outstanding teaching, research, and adminis- 
trative staffs. 


New and nationally significant academic programs have 
been initiated under President Topping’s leadership, including 
the Urban Semester, degree programs in Urban Studies, and in- 
terdisciplinary programs in gerontology, in marine sciences, 
and in material science. 


Impressive new buildings have been erected for engineer- 
ing, medicine, dentistry, student activities, international and 
public affairs, education, business administration, biological 
research, and marine sciences. Still other structures are under- 
way or on the drawing boards. 


When Dr. Topping announced USC's Master Plan, he said 
the University would seek to raise $106-million-plus “‘as 
quickly as possible.’ That goal was reached in five years and 
now, eight years after the announcement, USC can report it 
has raised more than $153 million. Of that sum, an amount 
equal to the original dollar goal of the Master Plan has come 
from private contributions. 


Other universities took note of Dr. Topping’s leadership 
and conferred honorary degrees on him. Marking its 75th an- 
niversary, Occidental College conferred an LL.D. degree on 
Dr. Topping in April of 1962; the University of California fol- 
lowed with a similar honor at UCLA the same year, In 1963, 
USC conferred its own honorary doctor of laws degree on its 
President. Hebrew Union College honored him with the doctor 
of humane letters degree in 1966 and Loyola University of 
tos Angeles added an honorary LL.D. degree in 1968. 


In 1969, USC's President was named Construction Man of 
the Year at the Los Angeles Area Chamber of Commerce's Con- 
struction Industries banquet for spearheading the most ambi- 
tious and successful building program in USC's history. He was 
of the award.

JESS HILL 
Director of Athletics 


Jess Hill, former track and 
football coach at Troy, begins 
his 13th year as Director of 
Intercollegiate Athletics at the 
University of Southern Califor- 
nia. 

Born Jesse Terrill Hill in 
Yates, Missouri, on January 
20, 1907, Jess came west with 
his family ten years later and 
became one of the top ath- 
letes in Southern California 
while at Corona High School. 
From the fall of 1923 to the 
spring of 1925, he was award- 
ed ten letters in five sports—football, basketball, baseball, 
track and tennis. 

Following his graduation from Corona in 1925, Hill at- 
tended Riverside Junior College for one year, earning letters 
in football, basketball, baseball and track as Riverside fielded 
championship teams in all four sports. 

Jess entered USC in February, 1927 and continued to 
exhibit his all-around athletic prowess, playing football and 
baseball and compiling an outstanding track and field record. 
He became the first Trojan in history to surpass 25 feet in 
the broad jump, leaping 25’ 7%” to win the 1929 IC4A 
title. 

Also outstanding in his studies, Jess graduated cum laude 
following the 1930 collegiate baseball season. 

Hill signed immediately to play professional baseball with 
the old Hollywood Stars. In his first plate appearance, he hit 
one out of the park at Wrigley Field to start a ten-year 
career in Triple-A and Major League baseball, 

In the off-season during his professional baseball years, 
Jess launched his coaching career at Corona High. Just as 
in his playing days, he moved from Corona to Riverside JC. 
His next position was at Long Beach JC where he was on 
the physical education and coaching staff when World War II 
came. 

Hill enlisted in the Navy in March of 1942 and served until 
February of 1946 when he was separated from active duty 
with the rank of Lieutenant Commander, 

Jess returned to USC in the fall of 1946 to begin a period 
of ten years of advancement within the athletic department. 
In 1948 he was selected as assistant track coach, being 
groomed to succeed Dean Cromwell as head track coach after 
the latter's retirement at the end of the ‘48 season. 

Hill had two undefeated years, 1949 and 1950, as head 
track coach at USC, continuing the great Trojan track heritage 
and winning two National Championships. He also held that 
post in 1962 following the sudden death of his close friend, 
Jess Mortensen. 

Early in 1951 he was named head football coach, follow- 
ing the resignation of Jeff Cravath. 

Hill's six-year record as head football coach at USC was 
45 wins, 17 losses, and 1 tie for a .722 winning percentage. 

Jess is married to the former Elizabeth Helen Glass. They 
have two children, Mrs. Mary Bett Carter, a USC graduate, 
and Jess Jr., also a USC graduate.

JOHN McKAY 
Head Football Coach 


The John McKay era at the 
University of Southern Califor- 
nia has been one seldom 
equalled in the annals of col- 
legiate football — particularly 
the record of the Trojans dur- 
ing the past seven seasons. 

In the last seven years, USC 
has won two national titles, 
finishing in “the “'top ten’’ rat- 
ings on six occasions, won five 
Pacific-8 titles and appeared in 
the biggest of all bow! games, 

Y, An the Rose Bowl, four times. 
i USC's 19-2-1 record in the past 
two years is the best in the nation. 

John McKay, 45, dubbed the Silver Fox by Los Angeles 
sportswriters, is recognized as one of the most inventive foot- 
ball coaches in the business. It is McKay who is generally 
credited with popularizing the ‘'l’’ formation, now called the 
Shifting-T on most fronts. 

McKay, one of the nation’s foremost football clinic and af- 
ter dinner speakers, sprinkles his football lectures with wit 
and humor and is in constant demand for public appearances. 


In the last seven seasons, McKay coached teams have won 
58, lost 14 and tied 2. Only Alabama has a better won-loss 
record in the nation over that period of time. USC has won 
five conference titles and finished second in the Pacific-8 on 
the other four occasions in McKay's nine year tenure at the 
University. His total nine-year record is 66-25-3. 


Conference-wise the Trojans are 35-7-1 under McKay, an 
.833 winning percentage. His teams have never lost to Stan- 
ford or California and he holds a 6-3 edge over cross town 
rival, UCLA. 


McKay's first national title came in 1962, his third season 
as Trojan head man. That club went 11-0 and defeated Wis- 
consin, 42-37, in the 1963 Rose Bowl game. Mike Garrett be- 
came USC’s and John McKay's first Heisman Trophy winner 
in 1965 and the 1967 club won McKay's second national ti- 
tle and second Rose Bowl, 14-3, over Indiana. 


This past season it was O. J. Simpson who earned the 
Heisman Trophy, and McKay's second honoree in just four 
years. The two national titles under McKay were USC's first 
since the pair captured by Howard Jones’ teams in 1928 and 
1931, McKay was honored by his fellow coaches as the 
American Football Coaches Assn. ‘Coach of the Year"’ in 1962. 

McKay's coaching honors are many. He has twice been 
named Coach of the Year by the Columbus Touchdown Club. 
He has been likewise honored by the Washington DC Touch- 
down Club, both wire services and in 1967 was named 
Sportsman of the Year by the Houston Touchdown Club. 


McKay was born July 5, 1923 in Everettsville, West Vir- 
ginia, and received his secondary education at Shinnston High 
School, graduating in 1941. As a prep, he was accorded All- 
State honors in both basketball and football, Following his 
graduation from high school, he entered the Air Force and 
saw action as a tail gunner with the 20th Air Force in the 
Pacific. 


A 1950 graduate of the University of Oregon, McKay 
8 


capped a brilliant college career by leading the 1948 Web- 
foots in scoring and playing a key role in their march to the 
Cotton Bowl. In addition to being named to the All-Coast 
first team, he received All-America mention for the 1948 
campaign. 

Immediately following graduation, McKay began his coach- 
ing career as an assistant to coach Jim Aiken at his alma 
mater. When Len Casonova took over the Oregon coaching 
reins in 1951, he retained his assistant's post and remained 
through the 1958 season. During the summer of 1959, Mc- 
Kay joined the Trojan staff as backfield coach. Although a 
member of the staff for less than a year, he was a popular 


choice to replace Don Clark as USC head coach on Dec. 16, 
1959. 


In addition to the two Heisman Trophy winners McKay has 
coached 11 bona fide All-Americans including Marlin Mc- 
Keever, Hal Bedsole, Damon Bame, Bill Fisk, Nate Shaw, Ron 
Yary, Tim Rossovich and Adrian Young. 


The USC coach is married to the former Nancy Hunter, whom 
he met at Oregon. They have four children: Michele, 16; 
John Jr., 15; Theresa, 13 and Richard, 9. The McKays reside 
in Covina, a suburb of Los Angeles.

DAVE LEVY 


Assistant Coach 


Dave Levy is one of two assistant coaches who have been 
with John McKay since his first year—1960. Marv Goux is 
the other. 


levy came to USC from Long Beach Poly High School 
where he led the Jackrabbits to two CIF titles in three years 
and lost only one game. During his three-year tenure at 
Poly, Levy's teams compiled a 28-1-2 mark and captured the 
CIF “AAA” championship in 1958 and 1959. 

Born Oct. 25, 1932 in Carrollton, Missouri, Levy came to 
Long Beach as a child. He attended Long Beach Poly where 
he won All-City honors as a tackle in football and with the 
track team as a weight man. 


Following his graduation from high school in 1950, he 
attended Long Beach City College and played football for 
two years, Levy then transferred to UCLA and lettered in 
1952 and 1953 for the Bruins, playing in the 1954 Rose 
Bowl game against Michigan State. 

Graduating in 1954, Dave spent the 1954 football season 
as a UCLA freshman assistant. He moved on to Long Beach 
City College as a student assistant in 1955. 

Levy returned to his alma mater, Long Beach Poly, in 1956 
and served one season as line coach. He became head coach 
in 1957. Besides his imposing record, Levy was credited 
with producing several nationally ranked prep stars, such as 
tackle Jim Smith (1957), halfbacks Dee Andrews (1958) and 
Willie Brown (1959), fullback Lonzo Irvin (1959) and tackle 
Mike Giers (1959). 


Dave and his wife, Marlys, live in Long Beach. They have 
two children, John, 14 and Robert 10. 


JOE GIBBS 


Assistant Coach 


Named to the USC coaching staff in January, Joe Gibbs 
started his first season on the sidelines for the Cardinal and 
Gold this fall. 

Before joining the Trojans, Gibbs spent two seasons on the 
football staff at Florida State. In his second year at Florida 
State, Gibbs was the offensive chief for the Seminoles. 

Born in Mocksville, North Carolina on November 25, 1940, 
Gibbs moved to California in 1955 and attended Sante Fe 
Springs High School in Los Angeles where he was an all- 
league in football, baseball and basketball. 

After graduation, Gibbs attended Cerritos Junior College 
where he starred in football. After two years at Cerritos, he 
transferred to San Diego State. 

At San Diego State, Gibbs played center, guard and end. 
He graduated in 1963 and joined the Aztec coaching staff in 
1964 where he helped direct the club to the national college 
division championship in 1966. 

Gibbs left San Diego State in 1967 to accept a coaching po- 
sition with Florida State. 

Gibbs and his wife, Pat, and son J. D., live in Huntington 
Beach.

MARV GOUX 


Assistant Coach 


After serving as head freshman coach and assistant with 
the varsity team when he joined the staff in 1957, Marv 
moved up to the post of a fulltime varsity assistant prior’ to 
the 196] season. 

Marv was born September 8, 1932, in Santa Barbara. Still 
listed as an all-time prep great in his hometown, he was 
accorded both CIF and All-American honors. 

He spent the 1951 season at Ventura JC, where he again 
won All-American honors as a linebacker. Transferring to USC 
in 1952, he became an immediate varsity standout, starring 
as a linebacker under the two-platoon system and playing 
in the Rose Bowl win over Wisconsin. A back ailment kept 
him out of action in 1953, but the inspirational Goux came 
back stronger than ever in 1954 to take over the starting 
center job, although he had never played offensive center 
before. He won his third varsity letter in 1955. 

Goux, although relatively small for a lineman, made up 
for his size deficiency with tremendous desire and now ranks 
as one of the most inspirational footballers in Trojan history. 
He is one of only two USC gridders to twice win the Davis- 
Teschke Award as the most inspirational varsity footballer. 
Marv also co-captained the 1955 squad. 

Following his graduation, Marv took over the head coach- 
ing job at Carpinteria High School. The prep team posted a 
9 win, 1 loss record and went to the 1956 small school 
CIF finals. 

He is married to the former Barbara Groth and has a 
daughter Linda Marie, 11. They live in Encino. 


ROD HUMENUIK 


Assistant Coach 


When Rod Humenuik joined John McKay in June, 1966, 
he became the third former Trojan varsity player on the 
coaching staff. 

His homecoming came after a three-year career in Cana- 
dian football, two years in high school coaching and one 
season as line coach of the national junior college champion 
Fullerton Hornets. Unlike his fellow assistant coaches, Marv 
Goux and Craig Fertig, however, Humenuik was restricted to 
just two years on the Trojan varsity. After lettering in 1956 
and 1957 as a tackle, he had his senior year wiped out by 
the Pacific Coast Conference penalties. 

Born June 17, 1938 in Detroit, Humenuik began his foot- 
ball career at Los Angeles High where he was an All-City 
tackle in 1954. At Pierce College in 1955 he won All- 
Western State Conference and honorable mention JC All- 
America honors. He then transferred to USC. 

Humenuik graduated with a bachelor of science degree in 
1959 and received a master's degree in education in 1960. 

From USC he went to the Canadian Football League, 
starting at offensive guard for the Winnipeg Blue Bombers 
for three consecutive seasons {1960-62). 

Humenuik decided to take an assistant's post with La 
Habra High in 1963. The next season he was an assistant 
at Long Beach Poly as the Jackrabbits captured the Moore 
League crown, then took over as Fullerton line coach in 1965. 

Humenuik and his wife, Suzy, are residents of Whittier. 
They are the patents of three girls, Kimberly, 9; Kerry, 7, and 
Kelly, 4, and a young son, Mike, 1 month.

LARRY BRYAN 


Assistant Coach 


Larry Bryan joined the USC coaching staff in March, after 
spending three seasons at Bishop Amat High School. 


Bryan was born November 3, 1941 in Lafayette, Indiana 
and arrived in California in 1947, He attended Huntington 
Park High School where he was an All-City football selection 
at halfback in 1959. Bryan also participated in track during 
his high school career as a 100 and 440-yard sprinter. 


After high school, Bryan attended Glendale City College 
for one year and was named to the Junior College All-Ameri- 
ca squad for his running prowess. 


The new Trojan aide left Glendale for Utah State Univer- 
sity, where he was a three year starter at fullback. 


Following his playing days at Utah State, Bryan coached 
the University's freshman squad to a 7-1 mark over two sea- 
sons prior to joining the staff at Bishop Amat in 1966. 


Bryan and his wife, Gayle, and son Derek, three, reside 
in Downey. 


PHIL KRUEGER 


Assistant Coach 
Phil Krueger is now in his fourth year on the USC staff. 


He was born Oct. 6, 1929 in La Salle, Ill. At University 
City High School in Missouri, he quarterbacked the football 
team to two consecutive city championships and also lettered 
one year in basketball. 


Krueger then entered Southeast Missouri State College, re- 
ceiving bachelor’s degrees in English and physical education 
in 1951. He played quarterback for three years on the 
Southeast Missouri team. Upon graduating, Krueger coached 
one year as an assistant at Festus High School in Missouri. 
He then went in the army, was commissioned a first lieuten- 
ant and received the Bronze Star for meritorious service in 
Korea, 


The USC aide, who has taught both college and high 
school English, went back to the books when he was dis- 
charged from the army and got a master’s degree in educa- 
tion from Missouri in 1956. Krueger then returned to high 
school coaching, compiling a 24-13 mark in four years as 
head coach, 1956-59, at Berkeley (Missouri), Yuma (Arizona) 
and Beaumont {St. Louis). His 1958 Yuma team won the 
state title. 


Krueger then joined the Long Beach City College staff 
where he served for three years, 1960-62. Krueger went to 
Utah State in 1963. 


In 1964 and 1965 Krueger was head mentor at Fresno 
State and posted a 10-10 mark for two seasons. 


He and his wife, Kathleen, live in Long Beach and have a 
five-year-old daughter, Kristi.

CRAIG FERTIG 
Assistant Coach 


One of the heroes of USC’s memorable 20-17 victory 
over Notre Dame in 1964, Craig Fertig joined the Trojan foot- 
ball coaching staff in February of 1965, just one week fol- 
lowing his graduation. 

Fertig, the quarterback who tossed the winning touchdown 
pass to Rod Sherman to beat the Irish, was drafted by the 
NFL Pittsburgh Steelers after a successful senior year which 
saw him set eight varsity passing records. Those eight marks 
include single season records in most yards gained passing 
(1671), most passes completed (109), most passes attempted 
(209), and most yards total offense (1695); and single game 
records in most passes completed (21), most yards gained 
passing (371) most yards running and passing (348) and 
most touchdown passes (4). 

Fertig also ranks fifth among Trojan career passing 
leaders with 151 completions in 290 attempts for 2230 yards 
and 16 touchdowns. 

An All-City quarterback at Huntington Park High, Fertig 
led his team to the Los Angeles city crown, defeating a 
Reseda team quarterbacked by former USC All-American end 
Hal Bedsole, 46-6. 

Fertig, a physical education major, entered USC in the 
fall of 1960. During his sophomore and junior years at the 
Univelsity he was overshadowed by Pete Beathard and Bill 
Nelsen, but finally was given the starting job uring the latter 
part of the 1963 season when Beathard was injured. 

He had a tremendous senior year, out-dueling All-American 
Craig Morton of California and John Huarte of Notre Dame, 
as the Trojans rolled to a 7-3 campaign, climaxed by the 
exciting win over the previously unbeaten Irish. 

Craig and his wife Nancy, also a USC graduate, live in 
Glendale. They have one son, Marc John. 


WILLIE BROWN 


Assistant Coach 


Former Trojan standout Willie Brown joined the USC 
coaching staff in 1968 as an assistant coach on the football 
and baseball staffs. 

Brown was born March 21, 1942 in Tuscaloosa, Ala- 
bama, and began his football career at Long Beach Poly 
High. He is regarded as one of the greatest football players 
ever developed in Southern California prep circles, being 
named ‘CIF Player of the Year'' and prep All-American on 
Poly's CIF championship squad of 1959. 

Upon graduation from high school, the much sought-after 
halfback followed his prep mentor Dave Levy to USC. 

Brown was the leading rusher on Troy's 1962 national 
championship football team and also was the leading batter 
{.352) on USC's 1963 baseball squad which won the NCAA 
crown. He co-captained the 1963 football squad and still 
holds the record for the longest run from scrimmage, 93- 
yards vs. SMU in 1961. He was named USC's outstanding 
back in 1962 and most inspirational player in ‘63. 

From USC, Brown went into professional football for three 
years, playing for the Los Angeles Rams in 1964 and 1965, 
then moved over to the Philadelphia Eagles for the 1966 
season. 

Brown and his wife Carole, are now living in the city of 
Carson. They have two children, Brian, 6, and Kimberly, 4.

RACE TO THE ROSES 


The Pacific-8 Rose Bowl Representative is selected through 
execution of the following selection procedures: 


The Rose Bowl representative from the Pacific-8 shall be 
the conference champion automatically unless the conference 
champion is tied in the loss and tie column with respect to 
conference games with the funner-up or runners-up teams. 
If the conference champion (or co-champions) is (or are) tied 
in the loss and tie column, and the tied teams have played 
each other, the winner of that game will be the representa- 
tive. If there should be more than two teams involved, only 
the top two teams on the point system are to be considered. 
If the tied teams have not played one another, or have played 
@ tie game against each other, the Rose Bowl team from the 
Pacific-8 will be selected by the following formula: 


1. A conference win will be worth 4 points. 

2. A non-conference win will be worth 3 points. 
3. A conference tie will be worth 2 points. 

4. A non-conference tie will be worth 1 VY, points. 


The team receiving the largest total number of points for 
the entire season's play will be the Pacific-8 representative in 
the Rose Bowl. 


After the total points are ascertained for each team if a 
tie should occur between two or more teams, the team or 
teams last appearing in the Rose Bowl shall be eliminated. 


(Pacific-8, May, 1967)

Street, Los Angeles, California 90014.

statistics, coaches’ quotes and the box score will be distributed 
to press and radio. Play-by-play announcements are broadcast 
throughout the press box.

pictures from the sidelines and from atop the press box.

service will be allowed to operate between the restraining 
lines. A special armband must be worn by those photogra- 
phers entitled to shoot between the 35-yard lines. After the 
first quarter no photographer is allowed between the 35-yard 
lines.

RATING THE 1969 TEAM: 


In rating the 1969 USC varsity offensively | would say 
that our primary concern is the youthfulness of the back- 
field. We have very few experienced players returning and 
it will be difficult to replace the likes of O. J. Simpson, 
Steve Sogge, Danny Scott and tight ends Bob Klein and Bob 
Miller. 

Our strength should lie in our split receivers where we 
have three fine players in Gary Orcutt, Sam Dickerson and 
Terry DeKraai. They, along with Bob Chandler, form the best 
set of receivers we've had in my 10 years here. 

We have eight players returning who started at one time 
or other defensively. We would hope that the year's ex- 
perience would benefit us this season. 


OUTSTANDING PLAYERS: 


Offensively, Bob Chandler at flanker should be a good one. 
Sid Smith has already been selected on some of the All- 
America teams at tackle and is an outstanding drive blocker. 
Steve Lehmer and Fred Khasigian give us experienced guards 
who have started for two seasons and our quarterbacks are 
all good passers. 

Defensively, All-American candidates Jimmy Gunn, Al 
Cowlings and Sandy Durko will bear watching. Tony Terry 
and Gary McArthur are two more veteran defensive lineman 
we are counting on. 


OUTSTANDING NEWCOMERS: 


With the offensive backfield losses of Sogge, Simpson and 
Scott we will see several new faces including quarterbacks 
Jimmy Jones and Jim Fassel, fullback Charlie Evans, and 
halfbacks Clarence Davis and Lou Harris. We are expecting 
sophomore tackle John Vella to have an outstanding year for 
us, too.

Trojan football for eight years has presented one of the 
nation's most exciting offenses with the shifting-T formation first 
introduced by Coach John McKay in 1961. Troy again will 
employ the same attack with minor modifications during the 
1969 season. The following is a brief description of the 
Shifting-T offense,

HIGHLIGHTS OF THIS SHIFTING-T FORMATION 


There will be numerous variations to the above general for- 
mation, with the split (or X) end flopping-over to the right side 
occasionally. 

The split (or X) end will always be split. The man normally 
designated as the left end will always be the split or X end. 


The Y (or right) end will always line-up tight next to his 
tackle. Depending on what side the X end splits out, the tackles 
and guards will be designated as strong or weak tackles and 
guards, with the tackle and guard on the side of the split end 
being designated as the weak side and the tackle and guard on 
the side where the Y end plays in tight being designated as 
strong fackle and guard. 

The fullback and remaining back (normally the left halfback) 
will shift into a stance approximately two-yards behind and to 
the side of the quarterback. 

The fullback will normally shift to the right side of the 
quarterback. 

The flanker back (normally the right halfback) will be referred 
to as the Z back.

HUSKERS DISPLAY SOLID DEFENSE 


The key to Nebraska's success during the 1969 football season 
rests with the Huskers’ offense. Defensively, Coach Bob Devaney's club 
looks very solid with nine returning reaulars but the picture is 
somewhat muddled on the offensive ledger.

veteran Frank Patrick and newcomer Van Brownson to handle the 
all-important signal calling spot. 

Speedster Joe Orduna at halfback and tight end Jim McFarland 
are the strong points of the offense. McFarland is rated as one of 
the best in the country. 

The veteran defensive unit is led by a strong secondary featuring 
Dana Stepenson and A! Larson, Up front, Ken Geddes, Bill Hornbacher 
and Sherwin Jarmon are a talented trio. 

Overall, Devaney will have 31 lettermen available and some strong 
talent off a good freshman unit. But the success or failure of the 
Huskers definitely rests with the offensive unit, specifically the 
offensive line and quarterback position.

WILDCATS FEATURE AERIAL ATTACK 


Although Northwestern will enter the 1969 campaign with 11 
returning starters, Coach Alex Agase is leaning heavily on a talented 
freshman club to provide needed depth for another gruelling 10-game 
schedule. 

Last year the Wildcats set a school record by throwing 312 passes 
and that mark could be threatened this season according to Agase. 
‘We have some highly skilled athletes to handle both the throwing 
and catching,’’ says Agase. 

Expected to handle the throwing is junior quarterback Dave 
Shelbourne who developed into an outstanding signal-caller as a 
sophomore. Shelbourne completed 105 of 251 throws for 1,358 yards 
and 7 touchdowns. Shelbourne is being backed up by sophomore 
Maurice Daigneau who actually pushed for the number one spot in 
the spring. 

Regulars Kenny Luxton and Bruce Hubbard will handle the wide 
receiving chores and are backed by a couple of speedy sophomores, 
Jerry Brown and Barry Pearson. 

The Wildcats’ running game also looks better this time around 
but the interior line needs to be rebuilt. 

Defensively, the linebacking crew is the strongest point. Don Ross, 
Ray Forsthoffer and Joel Hall are all good ones. Sophomores will be 
given the opportunity in the secondary and front wall. 

Northwestern's schedule is awesome again. Besides the usual Big 
10 group, the Wildcasts face USC, California, UCLA and Notre Dame.

ATTENDANCE RECORDS 


Accurate attendance records have been compiled on Trojan football 
games for the past 49 seasons. During this timt 24,287,681 people 
have attended USC games. It is estimated that 500,000 saw the Trojan 
games from 1888 to 1919, for a total of more than 24 million since 
USC started playing intercollegiate football 80 years ago. 

Largest single-game crowd in USC history was the estimated 123,000 
that jammed Soldier Field in’ Chicago for the 1929 USC-Notre Dame 
game. Largest home crowd was 104,953 for the 1947 USC-Notre Dame 
game in the Coliseum.
